Counting, checking and reading from images, measured on your own lighting.

What is computer vision?

Computer vision uses cameras and models to count objects, inspect items, read text or identify conditions from images, with accuracy that depends heavily on the lighting, angle and image quality of the specific place it runs.

Computer vision demonstrations are convincing because the images are good. Real deployments live on whatever the existing camera produces, in whatever light the building has at four in the afternoon.

That gap is most of the risk. A model reporting ninety five percent accuracy on a benchmark can perform very differently on your loading bay, and the only way to know is to test on your images.

The applications that work well here are narrow and countable. Counting units, reading plates, checking whether a required item is present, flagging visible damage. Broad visual judgement is a much weaker proposition.

Frequently asked questions about computer vision

Will it work in our conditions?

That is exactly what we test before quoting. Benchmark accuracy tells you nothing about your site at four in the afternoon.

What if our images are not good enough?

We say so. We have told clients the conditions were not workable, because taking the project anyway would waste their money.

Do we need new cameras?

Sometimes, and it is often the cheapest improvement available. Software cannot recover information the image never captured.

Can it use our existing CCTV?

Frequently yes, though position and resolution decide it, and we assess that on your actual footage.

What tasks work best?

Narrow countable ones. Counting units, reading plates and codes, checking presence, flagging visible damage.

What does not work well?

Broad visual judgement. Asking a model to assess something subjective is a much weaker proposition and we would say so.

What happens with a blurred or awkward image?

The result goes to a person. Routing an uncertain image to a human is correct behaviour rather than a failure.

What accuracy will we actually get?

A figure measured on your own images, including when it is lower than you hoped, because trusting a vision system beyond its accuracy is what causes the damage.

Can it read number plates?

Yes, and yard and gate operations are among the more reliable applications, given reasonable camera angles.

Does it work at night?

Depends entirely on lighting, and that is one of the first things we test rather than assume.

How long does a vision project take?

Six to twelve weeks, starting with images from your actual site rather than a specification.

Does it need internet?

Not necessarily. Edge deployment runs on site where bandwidth or latency requires it.

Can it count produce or livestock?

Yes, and agricultural counting is a common request, though accuracy varies considerably with how the items are presented.

Will accuracy stay the same?

No, and that is why we monitor it. Cameras get dirty and lighting changes, and accuracy drifts with them.

Can it identify people?

We scope facial recognition very carefully and often decline it, since it raises data protection questions most clients have not considered.

Does it integrate with our systems?

Yes, writing counts and results into your existing records rather than sitting apart as another screen.

What about privacy of footage?

Images stay in your infrastructure. Where processing happens on site, they need not leave the building at all.

Can it detect safety violations?

Some, such as missing protective equipment, though we would be clear that it supports supervision rather than replacing it.

Who owns the trained model?

You do, along with the labelled images, which are the harder asset to recreate.

What does a computer vision project cost?

Camera count and task complexity determine it, quoted after the image assessment.

Glossary of terms

Computer vision terms in plain language.

Object detection
Locating and identifying specific items within an image.
Confidence threshold
The certainty level below which a result is sent to a person instead of accepted.
False positive
Reporting something that is not there, which is usually less costly than missing something that is.
Edge deployment
Running the model on site rather than sending images to a server.
Training images
Examples from your own conditions used to teach the model what to look for.
Drift
Accuracy declining over time as cameras, lighting or conditions change.
