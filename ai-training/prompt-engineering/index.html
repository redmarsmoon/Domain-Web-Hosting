Prompt Engineering Training

A small real skill, taught honestly, rather than a discipline with a certificate.

What is prompt engineering?

Prompt engineering is the practice of giving AI tools context, examples and constraints so their output needs less correction, and it is a genuinely useful skill that is considerably smaller than the market presents it as.

Most of the quality difference comes from three things: supplying the relevant context, showing an example of what good looks like, and stating the constraints. That is the core, and it can be taught in an afternoon.

The rest of what gets sold as prompt engineering is either superstition or techniques that mattered on older models and no longer do. Phrases people repeat because a result improved once are common and mostly meaningless.

It is also a skill with a shelf life. Models get better at inferring what you want, so the techniques that matter today are fewer than two years ago and will be fewer again.

Frequently asked questions about prompt engineering

Is prompt engineering a real skill?

Yes, and a smaller one than the market presents. Context, examples and constraints are most of it and they fit in an afternoon.

Should we hire a prompt engineer?

Almost certainly not. It is a skill your existing staff can learn in half a day rather than a role, and the market is quietly removing it.

What actually improves output most?

Showing an example of what good looks like. It is more effective than any wording technique and it is consistently underused.

Do the phrases people add actually help?

Mostly not. Repeated phrases that improved something once are superstition, and we identify and drop them.

Why is the skill shrinking?

Because models are getting better at inferring intent. Techniques that mattered two years ago are already unnecessary.

How long should training take?

Half a day. Anybody offering considerably more is padding, and we would rather say that than fill the time.

Will this fix all our output problems?

No. Some need retrieval or a different tool entirely, and we teach people to tell the difference rather than reword endlessly.

Can it be taught on our own tasks?

Yes, and it should be. A technique demonstrated on an example rarely survives contact with a real piece of work.

What about very long prompts?

Usually a sign of accumulated superstition. Long prompts often work despite their length rather than because of it.

Do different tools need different techniques?

Less than people think. Context, examples and constraints work across all of them, which is why we teach those.

Should we standardise prompts across the team?

For repeated tasks, yes. Shared prompts for common work remove most of the variation between people.

What is a system prompt?

Standing instructions applied to every request in a tool, which is worth setting properly once rather than repeating each time.

Is there value in a certificate?

Little, in our view. We would rather your people could do the work than hold a credential for a shrinking skill.

Does it help with structured output?

Yes, and requesting a specific format matters most when the result feeds a document or another system.

Can non technical staff learn it?

Easily. It is a communication skill rather than a technical one, and clear writers tend to do well at it.

Do the same techniques work when prompting in Swahili?

Yes, the three techniques carry across, though model quality varies by language and we would test on your material.

Will this help with our AI systems we built?

Partly. Prompts inside a product should be version controlled and tested, which is engineering rather than training.

How do we keep the skill current?

Mostly you do not need to. The direction of travel is that less technique is required, not more.

Do you record what our staff produce?

No. Work brought into the session belongs to your team and is not retained.

What does prompt training cost?

Group size determines it, quoted after the needs conversation. It is half a day and we price it as such.

Glossary of terms

Prompting terms in plain language.

Context
The background information supplied with a request, which affects results more than wording.
Few shot
Including examples of what good output looks like, which is the most effective single technique.
Constraint
A stated limit on length, format, audience or content.
Structured output
Requesting a specific format so the result can be used by another system.
Iteration
Going back and forth to improve a result, usually a symptom of an underspecified first request.
System prompt
Standing instructions applied to every request in a tool, as opposed to a single message.
