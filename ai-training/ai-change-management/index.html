AI Change Management

The hard part is not the software. It is what people think it means for them.

What is AI change management?

AI change management handles the human side of introducing AI, including the job security question, the people whose roles change, and the difference between consulting staff and informing them.

Staff resistance to AI is usually treated as a communication problem. It is normally a straightforward calculation: people are working out whether this makes their job easier or makes them unnecessary.

Reassurance does not settle that, because nobody believes a reassurance from the organisation that would also make the decision. Only actual decisions, communicated early, change the answer.

The organisations that get this right decide what happens to affected roles before announcing anything, and say it plainly. The ones that get it wrong say nobody will lose their job, and are believed until the first restructure.

What we will not say to your staff

Duara handles the people side of AI projects from Nairobi, usually alongside an implementation, occasionally after one has stalled.

We will not help you tell staff their jobs are safe if you have not decided that. Being unable to answer is uncomfortable and survivable; saying something that turns out to be untrue destroys trust in everything else you announce for years.

Problems this solves

These are trust and honesty problems, which is why they do not respond to better slide decks.

  • error The job question goes unanswered. Nobody has said what happens to affected roles, so people assume the worst and act accordingly.
  • error Reassurance nobody believes. Staff are told their jobs are safe by the organisation that would also make the decision to cut them.
  • error Consultation after the decision. People are asked for input on something already agreed, which is quickly recognised and resented.
  • error Middle managers left out. Supervisors hear about the change with their teams and cannot answer questions, which undermines them and the project.

How the process works, step by step

Change work usually runs alongside a project for its duration, and it starts before any announcement rather than after the first complaint.

  1. Step 1

    Free impact review

    We establish which roles actually change, which is often fewer than staff fear and different from what management assumes.

  2. Step 2

    The employment decision

    You decide what happens to affected roles before anything is said, since that answer shapes everything else.

  3. Step 3

    Managers first

    Supervisors are briefed and given answers before their teams hear anything.

  4. Step 4

    Announcement and consultation

    The change is communicated with real input still possible rather than as a decision to be accepted.

  5. Step 5

    Involvement through delivery

    Affected staff stay involved in design, which improves the result and the reception.

Frequently asked questions about AI change management

Why do staff resist AI projects?

Because they are working out whether it makes their job easier or makes them unnecessary. That is a calculation rather than a misunderstanding.

Can we just reassure people?

Not credibly. Nobody believes a reassurance from the organisation that would also make the decision, so only actual decisions change the answer.

What if we have not decided about jobs yet?

Say that. Not knowing yet is survivable. A reassurance that later proves untrue damages every announcement you make for years.

Will you help us announce something untrue?

No. That statement outlives the project, and helping you make it would harm you more than the awkwardness of honesty now.

Who should hear first?

Managers, ahead of their teams. A supervisor blindsided in front of staff loses standing and the project loses its advocates.

Is consultation worth doing?

Only if decisions are genuinely still open. Asking for input on something settled is recognised immediately and costs more than not asking.

Should affected staff help design it?

Yes. They usually know where the plan will fail, and asking them is cheaper than discovering it during rollout.

What if resistance is actually justified?

We say so. Where opposition is a rational response to a bad plan, treating it as a communication problem wastes everybody time.

Do roles usually change as much as feared?

Rarely. The impact review normally finds fewer roles affected than staff fear and different ones than management assumed.

How do we handle redundancy honestly?

By deciding early, communicating clearly and treating people properly. There is no version of this that is comfortable, only versions that are honest.

What about union or staff representatives?

Involve them early. Late involvement converts a negotiation into a dispute, which costs far more time than the early conversation.

How long does change work take?

It runs alongside the project throughout, starting before any announcement rather than after the first complaint.

Can this fix a project that has already stalled?

Often, though it is harder. Trust lost through a bad announcement takes considerably longer to rebuild than to protect.

Do we need this if the project is small?

Less formally, though the job question arises even on small projects and is worth answering before it is asked.

What if managers are the resistant ones?

That is common and it usually means they have not been told what it means for them either.

How do we know if it is working?

Concerns being raised openly rather than privately is the clearest early signal, and silence is rarely a good sign.

Will staff tell us the truth in a session?

Some, and more where a route to raise concerns exists that does not go through their own manager.

Does this replace training?

No, they are different. Training builds skill and this settles whether people want to use it.

Who sees what staff say?

That is agreed in advance and honoured. Feedback promised as confidential must stay confidential or the channel dies.

What does change work cost?

Organisation size and project scope determine it, quoted after the impact review.

Glossary of terms

Change terms in plain language.

Impact assessment
Establishing which roles genuinely change before anything is announced.
Consultation
Seeking input while the decision is still open, as distinct from informing people afterwards.
Redeployment
Moving affected staff into other roles rather than losing them.
Cascade
The sequence in which news moves through an organisation, and who hears it first.
Resistance
Opposition to a change, which is frequently a rational response rather than a failure to understand.
Reskilling
Training that equips somebody for a different role rather than an improved version of the current one.
