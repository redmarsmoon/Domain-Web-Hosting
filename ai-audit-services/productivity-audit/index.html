Saved time that goes nowhere in particular has not been saved.

What is productivity audit?

A productivity audit measures whether AI tools an organisation pays for are genuinely used, how much time they save on specific real tasks, and whether that saved time reaches anything the business can point to.

Organisations buy AI tools, announce them, and rarely check afterwards. Licences renew because cancelling requires somebody to make a decision and renewal requires nobody to do anything at all.

When usage is measured the pattern is usually the same. A small number of people use the tools heavily, most stopped somewhere around week two, and the licence count reflects neither group.

The harder question is what the saved time became. Twenty minutes saved across a scattered day is absorbed into the day. Time only turns into money where it was consolidated into something the organisation can name.

Frequently asked questions about productivity audit

What does usage measurement usually find?

A small number of people use the tools heavily, most stopped around week two, and the licence count reflects neither group.

Are logins a good measure?

No. Opening a tool occasionally is a licence being paid for rather than a tool being used, and both look the same in vendor reporting.

Why not ask staff how much time they save?

Because people who like a tool report larger savings than it delivers, and the effect is big enough to change the conclusion.

How do you measure time saved?

On specific real tasks, timed with and without the tool, using enough instances that the number means something.

Why does it matter where saved time went?

Because twenty minutes recovered across a scattered day is absorbed by the day. Time becomes value only when it consolidates into something you can name.

Can saved time be real and invisible?

Yes, and that is the most common outcome. Staff genuinely feel faster and nothing in the numbers moves, and both are true.

Are heavy users always faster?

No, and that is the uncomfortable finding. Enthusiasm for a tool and performance with it are separate, and only one shows in usage statistics.

Should we train or cancel?

Drop off timing usually answers it. People who stopped in week two point at the tool, and people who never started point at the training.

Why do unused licences survive?

Because renewal requires nobody to act and cancellation requires a decision, so the default runs unopposed year after year.

Would you tell us to cancel a tool we champion?

Yes, where the evidence says so. It is an awkward finding to deliver and usually the one with the clearest value attached.

Is this about individual performance?

No, and we say so plainly. Staff who believe an audit feeds their appraisal stop producing usable information immediately.

What if we never baselined anything?

Common. Tasks can still be timed both ways now, which establishes the present position even where history is unavailable.

How many task instances do you need?

Enough to be meaningful, and where there are too few we say so rather than presenting a number with false precision.

How long does a productivity audit take?

Two to four weeks, with the task timing needing enough real instances to support a conclusion.

Does it cover tools built into software we already pay for?

Yes, and those are frequently the least measured, since nobody bought them separately and nobody tracks them.

Can it inform a renewal negotiation?

Yes. Knowing genuine usage by team is the strongest position anybody can take into a licence conversation.

What if usage is high but savings are not?

That is a real result and worth knowing. A tool people enjoy is not automatically a tool that pays for itself.

Does this replace an ROI review?

No. This measures usage and task time. Whether the overall investment paid is a wider question with its own method.

Who sees the usage data?

Whoever you nominate, reported by team rather than by individual.

What does a productivity audit cost?

The number of tools and teams in scope determines it, quoted after scoping.

Glossary of terms

Productivity measurement terms in plain language.

Adoption
Whether a tool became part of the work, as distinct from whether it was issued to somebody.
Depth of use
How substantially a tool is used, which login counts cannot show.
Drop off
When people stopped using a tool, usually a better diagnostic than how many still do.
Absorbed saving
Recovered time that scattered across a day and reached nothing the business can point to.
Consolidated time
Saved time gathered into something identifiable, which is the only form that turns into value.
Licence reconciliation
Comparing what is paid for with what is used, which frequently differ substantially.
