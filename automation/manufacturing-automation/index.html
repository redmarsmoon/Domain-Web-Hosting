Manufacturing Automation

Software around your existing plant, not a machinery replacement programme.

Book a free strategy call Call 0723658888

What is manufacturing automation?

Manufacturing automation in this sense means the software layer around production, recording output and downtime as it happens, tracking material consumption, capturing quality checks and producing the numbers a plant manager needs, without replacing machinery.

Manufacturing automation usually means robots and new machinery. That is a capital project with a long payback, and it is not what most Kenyan manufacturers need first.

The cheaper gap is information. Production is recorded on a clipboard, downtime is remembered rather than logged, and by the time a shift summary reaches a manager the reasons have blurred into everybody being busy.

Capturing what actually happened on the line, as it happens, costs a fraction of new plant and usually reveals that the constraint is not the machine anybody assumed.

Why your equipment budget is not our concern

Duara builds the software layer around production from Nairobi for food processors, packaging plants, light assembly and general manufacturing.

We do not sell machinery and we have no interest in whether you buy any. That matters because the honest answer is often that your bottleneck is changeover time or material availability rather than machine speed, and nobody selling equipment is well placed to tell you that.

Problems this solves

These are measurement gaps, and they are why plant decisions get made on impression rather than evidence.

  • error Production recorded on paper. Output is written on a clipboard and entered later, so nobody knows the real figure until the following day.
  • error Downtime reasons lost. A stoppage is remembered vaguely by the end of the shift, which makes the pattern invisible and the cause unfixable.
  • error Material variance found late. Consumption against standard is discovered at stock take, weeks after the batch that caused it.
  • error Quality checks on paper. Checks are recorded in a book nobody analyses, so a drift toward the limit is only noticed once it crosses.

Features

  • tune Downtime reasons at the moment. The reason is captured when the machine stops, which is the only point at which it is accurate.

Benefits

  • trending_up The real constraint becomes visible. Downtime data usually shows the bottleneck is changeover or material rather than the machine everybody blamed.
  • trending_up Variance is caught in hours. Material problems surface while the batch is still traceable rather than at the next count.

Technologies we use

  • memory Line side capture. Tablet or terminal recording of output, downtime and checks at the point of work.
  • memory Machine reading where possible. Taking counts directly from equipment that exposes them, rather than asking an operator to retype.
  • memory Downtime coding. A short structured reason list, kept deliberately brief so it is actually used.
  • memory Material consumption tracking. Recording issue and usage per batch against the standard recipe or bill of materials.
  • memory Shift and OEE reporting. Producing availability, performance and quality figures from recorded events.

How the process works, step by step

Manufacturing automation usually takes six to ten weeks, and it goes live on one line before anywhere else.

  1. Step 1

    Free plant review

    We look at how production and downtime are recorded now and what the numbers are used for.

  2. Step 2

    One line first

    A single line proves the capture design, because an interface operators reject is the failure mode that matters.

  3. Step 3

    Downtime codes agreed

    The reason list is built with supervisors and kept short, since a long list gets answered with the first option.

  4. Step 4

    Capture running

    Operators record at the line, and we watch how long it actually adds to a cycle.

  5. Step 5

    Extended and reported

    Other lines follow, and shift and OEE reporting is switched on once the data is complete.

Frequently asked questions about manufacturing automation

Is this about buying robots?

No. This is the software layer around your existing plant. Machinery is a capital project with a long payback and it is rarely what is needed first.

Do you sell equipment?

No, and that matters. Our answer is often that your bottleneck is changeover or material rather than machine speed.

What hardware do we need?

Tablets or terminals at the line. Where a machine exposes its counts we read them directly, and where it cannot we say so.

Will operators actually use it?

Only if it is faster than the clipboard, which is why we measure the time it adds to a cycle rather than assuming it is negligible.

What if operators reject it?

That is the failure mode that matters, which is why we prove the design on one line before going near the rest of the plant.

Why keep the downtime code list short?

Because a long list gets answered with whatever is first, producing data that looks complete and means nothing.

Can it calculate OEE?

Yes, from recorded events rather than estimates, which is usually the first time the figure is trustworthy.

Does it track material usage?

Per batch against your bill of materials, so variance shows up in hours rather than at the next stock take.

Can it record quality checks?

Yes, with measurements rather than just pass and fail, so drift toward a limit is visible before it is breached.

Will it connect to our machines?

Where they expose data, yes. Plenty of older equipment does not, and we are direct about that rather than promising a retrofit.

How long before one line is live?

Six to ten weeks, with the first line proving the capture design before anything is extended.

Will production data reach our ERP?

Yes, feeding output and consumption into your existing system rather than creating a separate record beside it.

Can it handle several shifts?

Yes, with reporting by shift, which frequently shows differences nobody had quantified.

Will it show which line performs best?

Yes, on the same measures, though we would caution that comparing lines running different products needs care.

Does it help with traceability?

Yes, batch level material and output records support recall traceability, which matters most in food and pharmaceutical work.

What about maintenance?

Downtime data feeds it, and recurring stoppages on one machine are usually the clearest maintenance signal available.

Is this AI?

Very little of it. Recording and calculating have right answers, and rules are what you want on a production line.

Can supervisors change the codes?

Yes. The reason list is yours to maintain, and it should be revisited as the plant changes.

Who owns the production data?

You do. It stays in your systems, is not exported to us, and trains no public AI model.

What does manufacturing automation cost?

Line count and hardware needs determine it, quoted after the plant review.

Glossary of terms

Production terms in plain language.

OEE
Overall Equipment Effectiveness, combining availability, performance and quality into one figure.
Downtime code
The short structured reason recorded when production stops.
Changeover
The time to switch a line from one product to another, frequently the real constraint.
Yield
Good output as a proportion of material consumed.
Bill of materials
The standard list of inputs required to make one unit of a product.
Variance
The difference between standard and actual material consumption.
